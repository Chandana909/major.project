# The base papers in the code

What each of the two base papers describes, where AuditIQ implements it, and the measurement that shows it
works. The descriptions come from the papers' published abstracts; both full texts are behind the publisher's
paywall, so the team still has to read them in full (RESULTS.md, "Before the paper is submitted").

## AuditBench (Wang et al., 2025)

A benchmark of large language models on financial statement auditing: real financial tables paired with
synthesized transaction data into which errors are injected, and a five-stage evaluation. The paper finds that
models identify errors when they are given the transaction history, but are weak at explaining the errors and at
citing the relevant standard, and struggle to complete a full audit and to make the required revisions.

| What AuditBench describes | In AuditIQ | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic transactions with injected, labelled errors | `auditiq_bench/`: procure-to-pay ledgers (vendors, purchase orders, goods receipts, invoices, approvals) with 14 anomaly classes injected and labelled at injection, plus hard negatives; an independent validator recomputes every label | DATASETS.md; ledgers from 5,000 to 500,000 invoices, regenerated byte for byte (RESULTS.md, Section 2) |
| Real financial tables beside the synthetic data | Not available: every ledger is synthetic (no organisation's records could be used) | RESULTS.md, Section 11 |
| Identifying the errors | The twelve controls (rule-defined classes) and the agents (judgement classes); detection measured per class | RESULTS.md, Sections 4 and 7 |
| Explaining an error | Every finding carries a message and the field values it rests on; an agent's finding is checked against the evidence it was shown (`scoring.grounding`) | Audit packs; "findings naming a violated rule" (Sections 7.2 and 7.3) |
| Citing the standard it violates | Every finding names its rule, and a control's finding also the page of the policy document (`policy_ingest.clause_citations`). For the controls this holds by construction; for the agents it is measured | 0.375 and 0.493 of the agents' findings name a violated rule on the 34- and 90-invoice samples (Section 7) |
| Completing a full audit | Every invoice gets a route (approve, sampled review, escalate), a risk score and an audit pack with a trail; whole ledgers are audited and stored | Section 3.6; the 500,000-invoice ledger (Section 5.1) |
| Making the required revisions | `auditiq/corrections.py`: the revision each control finding requires (amend the amount or quantity, re-approve by the right role, reject a duplicate, hold, obtain a document), computed from the evidence and the policy and shown in the audit pack and the web application | 1,200 findings on four ledgers, every one with a revision; 600 of 600 amendments and re-approvals resolve their finding without raising another (`results/revisions.md`, RESULTS.md Section 3.7) |
| Evaluating several models | The pipeline takes any OpenAI-compatible model (`AUDITIQ_LLM_MODEL`); one model, Qwen2.5-7B, has been measured | Section 7; a second model is plan item B10 |

## LLM-based multi-agent systems for financial markets (Liu et al., 2026)

A survey of multi-agent systems of language models for financial markets: heterogeneous, autonomous, adaptable
agents with bounded rationality; the mechanisms that produce collective intelligence; applications such as
market simulation, systemic risk analysis and policy evaluation; and technical and regulatory challenges.

| What the survey describes | In AuditIQ | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Heterogeneous agents with roles | Three specialist agents (amounts and approvals, tax identity, vendor intelligence) with remits taken from the policy's sections, a single-prompt auditor (C2) and a challenger (`auditiq/agents.py`) | Configurations C2 to C5 (Section 7) |
| Autonomy and bounded rationality | Each agent decides within its remit, from a bounded view: at most 40 of the vendor's other invoices per selection rule; a finding outside the remit is dropped | `results/history_cap.md`: no labelled case loses an invoice it depends on |
| Adaptability | The agents read the active policy's text, and the controls its registry: a new policy document changes both with no code change | Section 3 (0 wrong decisions in 1,360,000 audits) |
| Collective intelligence: interaction and debate | The challenger tests each agent finding against the evidence its author had and may remove it; confidence counts agreement over re-runs | C4 and C5 (Section 7): the challenger halves false alarms on hard negatives |
| Applications: policy evaluation | `auditiq/simulation.py`: before a policy document is adopted, every invoice of the stored ledger is audited under the active policy and under the document, in memory, and the difference is reported (decisions moved, routes, findings by rule, money newly held). In the API (`POST /api/policy/simulate`), the command line (`policy simulate`) and the policy page | On the 20,000-invoice ledger the dry run of the strict policy predicts 6,353 invoices for review, the number the adoption then produces; tests check that the prediction equals the audit (RESULTS.md, Section 3.8) |
| Applications: systemic risk | Risk aggregated per vendor: the risk map of the web application (every vendor, its findings, its highest risk, its invoices waiting) | The 3D risk constellation; `/api/risk-map` |
| Market simulation | Not applicable: AuditIQ audits transactions, it does not simulate markets | |
| Regulatory challenge: traceability | Every finding leads to a rule, a document, a page and the field values; every stage writes to the audit trail; results are never overwritten; a decision is recorded with the signed-in reviewer's name and role | Audit packs and trails; `api_users` and the roles of the API (SECURITY.md) |
| Technical challenge: reliability of agents | The controls decide the decidable rules before any model, and no model output can weaken them (Proposition 1 of the paper) | Section 4 and the paper |
